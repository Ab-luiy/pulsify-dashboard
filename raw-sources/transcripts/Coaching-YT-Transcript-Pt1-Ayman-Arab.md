# How to build a $100k/month consulting YouTube channel in 2026

> Source: Aean (Ayman Arab) — manages 40+ channels across info products and B2B services (drop shipping, tech, agency, cold email, etc.). Started making YouTube videos at 14.

## The core thesis

The common misconception: you have to choose between very broad top-of-funnel content (no conversions) or hyper-niche content (no traction). Both are wrong.

This system takes creators from **zero subscribers to $200k–$400k/month directly from YouTube with no paid ads** — and applies whether you're at $0 or already at $200k/month outside YouTube.

The five areas:

1. **Pre-production** — ideas and research
2. **Production** — packaging, scripting, filming, editing
3. **Post-production** — distribution and SEO
4. **Tracking** — data and metrics
5. **Systems** — team structure and consistency

---

## Part 1: Pre-production

### YouTube as an ecosystem

Stop thinking video-to-video. Before a prospect converts, they typically watch **4–5 videos or more**. Videos must lead into each other and function as an ecosystem — the same way an ad account has social proof ads, top-funnel ads, and educational ads, but in long-form organic format.

### The three content types

#### 1. Action-based videos (Top of Funnel) — 50% of content

Evergreen, searchable "how to" videos. Using a drop shipping coach as the example:
- How to pick a product
- How to build a store
- How to set up a Facebook ad account
- How to run ads

Break your service down into its component steps — each step becomes its own video. Over time, create derivatives of each: "How to come up with ideas using AI," "How to come up with ideas using [X]."

The goal is to capture the segment of the market already searching for these things in YouTube — these people are further along in the buying journey and more likely to purchase. Focus on evergreen content. **Think of each video as a property you collect rent from forever.**

#### 2. Conversion-focused content (Middle and Bottom of Funnel)

When views aren't converting to sales, the issue is a lack of conversion-focused content. Two formats:

**Client interviews:** Have the student or client state their transformation within the first 30 seconds. On a 10-minute video, average watch time is around 30%, so the **first three minutes are critical** for delivering key information.

**Case study breakdowns:** You don't need the student on camera. Break down the exact steps they took to achieve their result — 20k/month with drop shipping, 50k/month with an AI agency, etc. People naturally self-identify with case studies. If you're from New York and case study your success, you'll attract New York-based viewers. Add a case study from someone in New Mexico, and you open that market too. Always include a CTA toward your VSL funnel or webinar within these videos.

#### 3. The free course video (Signature content)

Every channel should have a **free course video**:
- 60–75 minutes
- Typically a screen recording
- CTA within the first 8 minutes pointing to your product
- Give away almost everything

The more value you give, the more buyers you get. People buy permission and support — they want to text you or your CSM and get reassurance. Giving value builds trust, and people who get results from your free content become your easiest closes. The majority of viewers won't actually implement what you teach anyway — they'll watch, feel productive, and eventually buy.

> **Real example:** One client went from $200k/month to $430k/month within one month of posting their free course.

The customer journey looks like:

```
Story video (who is this guy?)
  → Free course (he actually knows what he's talking about)
  → Specific how-to video
  → Case study breakdown
  → Book call
```

When someone calls having watched 3–4 of your videos, it's the easiest close in the world.

The free course also captures audiences from other creators. If someone watches another creator's top-of-funnel video and then finds your free course in search results, they may convert to your program instead.

### Finding and validating ideas

Forget TubeBuddy and most external tools — they're largely unnecessary. The only external metric worth using is the **outlier score** (in VidIQ and others), which shows how well a video performed relative to that channel's average. A video with a 10x outlier score on a channel averaging 1,000 views means it got 10,000 views — worth taking note of.

The real validation tool is the **YouTube search bar itself** — the second-largest search engine in the world.

Method:
1. Take each content pillar
2. Put yourself in the prospect's shoes
3. Type naturally: "how to find a product," "how to build a drop shipping store"
4. See what comes up, check the view counts, check outlier scores
5. If existing videos are getting strong views and traction → validated
6. If they're getting 100–200 views → skip

**Don't fear competition.** People are always looking for a new personality to watch — someone who looks like them, is more energetic, or more calm. Saturated topics still work.

### Pulling ideas from other niches

The biggest idea-generation mistake is pulling from direct competitors — you blend in. Instead, pull from:
- Other bizops (business opportunity niches)
- Then go one level further: completely unrelated niches like math, gaming, entertainment

Let others do the testing; you take the proven winners and apply them to your niche with your own spin.

**Example 1:** The tier-list/ranking format started with celebrities and Fortnite guns, moved into business content when Rob the Bank used it, and was then successfully adapted for growth operating — first in that niche to do it, drove a flood of high-quality booked calls.

**Example 2:** A challenge video format ("12 hours to make money") was adapted from an AI brand, spun into "I bet you can't land a client in 12 hours — I wagered $5k on it." Result: **61,000 views.**

The idea is already proven — you only need to figure out your spin.

## Part 2: Production

### Packaging (title + thumbnail)

Packaging means the combination of title and thumbnail working together. Most viewers look at thumbnails first; if the thumbnail is compelling enough, they read the title. **The thumbnail should lead them to the title — not repeat it.** The title and thumbnail complete each other.

**Thumbnail principles** (followed 90%+ of the time):

- Always include the creator's face — it builds mental real estate and trust
- 4 words or fewer on the thumbnail — more text means smaller font, harder to read on mobile (where 95% of traffic comes from)
- Background matters — a penthouse background signals higher authority than a messy bedroom
- Thumbnail must not repeat the title — it should complete it, visually
- Use recognizable icons when relevant — a Shopify logo on a drop shipping video, a ChatGPT logo on an AI video. This saves text space and signals immediately what the video covers. Familiar icons reduce psychological friction (like F1 logos — viewers know exactly what they're getting)

**Title principles:**

- Keep titles under 50–60 characters — anything beyond becomes an ellipsis on mobile; front-load the important part
- A longer title can still be useful for keyword capture even if it gets cut off on mobile

### Scripting

Two formats: word-for-word (best for teleprompter users) and outlines (preferred by most).

**With outlines:**
- Write the hook and outro word-for-word
- Use bullet points with a transition sentence for each new section in the body
- Since you already have expertise, you can elaborate naturally on each bullet

**Hook structure depends on audience:**

**B2B hooks** should be short and get straight to the point. Sophisticated business owners don't want long intros — they searched for something specific.

> Example: Spencer Pawhuska's 1–2 minute video on "how to find your post IDs on Facebook Ads" — only people doing well enough to need that information will find it, and 100 of those people watching is extremely valuable. Everyone who watches that video also gets naturally retargeted by the rest of the channel.

**B2C hooks** can be longer because the audience needs more convincing. Frameworks like "proof, promise, plan" (popularized by Hormozi) still work but are becoming overused.

For challenge videos:
- Tease the result without revealing it
- Blur revenue numbers
- Show charts to trigger dopamine
- Put the viewer in your shoes ("I started with only $500")

Whatever you showed in the title and thumbnail, you must deliver visually in the hook. If you mentioned money, show a Stripe or PayPal dashboard. Use payment platforms your specific audience recognizes (PayPal/Cash App for B2C, Stripe for B2B).

**Never clickbait** — bad watch time kills distribution. Watch time is the most important metric on YouTube.

**Body:** Use bullet-point questions as prompts, with a written transition sentence at the start of each new section. Elaborate naturally from your expertise.

**Outro:** End when you've finished your points — no fluff. Never say "Alright guys, that's everything for this video" — that phrase triggers immediate drop-off.

Instead, transition naturally into your CTA by framing the logical next step: "Now that you know how to find a product, the next thing you'll need is traffic — that's exactly what I cover inside my program. Link in description."

Give viewers two options:
1. Click to buy (for those already ready)
2. Point them to the next video in the funnel (for those still building trust)

### Editing

**Editing is the least important part of a video — but it still matters.** Match the editing style to the ICP:

**B2B:** Skip heavy animations. Use clean cuts, zoom-ins, and transitional elements that improve comprehension. The viewer cares whether you're well-presented, properly filmed, and organized — even if they just want the information.

**B2C:** More editing, especially in the hook. Heavy hook editing to capture attention, which then eases into the body. The goal is always comprehension and retention, not animation for its own sake.

> One account generating $200k/month from YouTube uses heavy hook edits and lighter body edits.

The editor's job is to keep the video flowing. **A poor editor can completely destroy an otherwise good video.** It takes skill to understand pacing and flow, not just cut-and-paste assembly.

## Part 3: Post-production

### Posting

Tags and descriptions no longer significantly impact rankings — YouTube changed this around 2019 after keyword stuffing created a poor viewer experience. YouTube now weighs **watch time, title, thumbnail, and transcript** heavily over tags.

What actually matters at upload:

- Title and thumbnail (already done)
- CTA link at the top of the description (or paste the title as line 1 and CTA as line 2 — both work; CTA first is the current preference)
- Pin a comment with the CTA link — many viewers scroll to comments while watching, so your link should be there
- Post around 11am–12pm Eastern, weekdays — but posting time doesn't matter nearly as much as consistency; late-night posts still perform
- Say your target keywords naturally in the video — YouTube indexes the transcript, so verbal keyword usage helps search ranking

### Promotion

After posting, promote on every platform: Instagram stories, email list, Twitter, anywhere you're active. **Ignore the advice to avoid external traffic — it actively helps.**

When someone who follows you on Instagram clicks through and watches a significant portion of your video, YouTube's algorithm identifies that viewer's interests and finds more people like them, amplifying organic reach.

## Part 4: Tracking

> Views are deceptive. A video with 2,000 views may generate no calls, while a video with 400 views books consistently. **Without tracking, you're flying blind.**

Use **Trackio** — built specifically for organic content attribution, unlike most tracking tools built for paid ads. Most paid-ad attribution tools only show last-click; Trackio shows the full customer journey. You can filter by "booked calls from YouTube" and see every video a prospect consumed before booking.

**Key metrics to track:**

- **CTR** (click-through rate on your CTA link) — tells you how many viewers were engaged enough to act, and whether your CTAs are strong enough
- **Booked calls per video** — which videos actually drive pipeline
- **Booked calls vs. clicks ratio** — prevents you from over-investing in high-view, low-conversion top-of-funnel content (e.g., vlogs)

The consistent pattern across all tracked accounts:

```
Top-of-funnel video → Free course → Value video → Case study → Book call
```

Once you can see this data yourself, you stop second-guessing the strategy. Tracking also tells you what type of video to make next:
- If offer clicks are dropping → post more middle or bottom-of-funnel content
- If clicks are high but conversions aren't → your offer or funnel may need attention

**Treat YouTube like math, not guesswork.**

## Part 5: Systems and team

### Team structure by stage

**At $10k/month:** Do everything yourself except editing and thumbnail design. You don't have enough margin yet to hire a full team.

**At $100k–$500k/month:** Add a creative director (strategist) who:
- Manages the editor and thumbnail designer
- Develops ideas
- Writes scripts
- Has a direct communication line with you

Market feedback from your students and clients should flow directly into content decisions. Scale volume by adding more editors as needed. That's the full structure.

### Why creators plateau

**Primary reason: they stop posting.**

Consistency is one of the most critical factors in YouTube growth — the algorithm rewards it. If time is the constraint, hire it out. **Not doing YouTube is actively costing you money.**

**Secondary reason: they become boring.**

YouTube is winner-take-all — the top 1–3 creators in each niche capture most of the audience. Blending in with safe, generic content means you lose. **Personality, differentiation, and spice are what separate the top creators from the forgotten ones.** Keep innovating, keep introducing new formats and angles, and never let complacency turn your channel into background noise.
