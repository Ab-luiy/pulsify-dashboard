## Where the packaging breaks

**Four-word compression test.** Titles 2, 3 and 5 pass (`survive losing weeks`, `tripled net worth`, `bounce back -$20,000`). Title 1 fails: the plus sign welds two promises together, so no four words carry it. Title 4 fails: `trading harder than necessary` names no concrete thing the viewer receives.

**Complete vs repeat.** All five are complete titles, which is the right call for an authority channel. Nothing to fix here.

**One clear focal point.** All five fail. Three are a room, and the two screen-recordings carry browser chrome, sidebars, a taskbar and a weather widget competing with the actual subject.

**Reads at 120px.** All five fail. No text, no crop, no deliberate contrast.

**Title and thumbnail connected but not redundant.** Not applicable, because there is no thumbnail text on any of them. The entire second half of the pair is missing, so the title is doing all the work alone.

**Face on camera.** Technically present in three, but as an accident of frame selection rather than a chosen expression. Two have no face at all.

**Topic overlap.** Videos 1, 2 and 5 are the same subject. Two of them make the same promise with different wording, so they compete with each other and drag the channel average down.

**What is already right.** His titles are written in practitioner language, not beginner language. "How to survive losing weeks" is what a funded trader says in his own head on a red afternoon. Most of the niche writes for people who want to get rich; he writes for someone already in the seat and already down. That instinct is the hard part and he has it.

## How every video on this channel gets structured

### The hook, first 30 seconds

Confirm, do not set up. Alexei's rule is that the opening 30 seconds confirms the exact promise the title made. "What's up guys, welcome back" is the failing version. Ayman adds that a money-motivated audience gets the short hook, and funded traders are as money-motivated as an audience gets.

The hook has three jobs and no more: confirm the promise, signal briefly why he is the one saying it, open a loop that only closes later in the video.

Ayman's specific mechanic: the hook must visually deliver what the packaging promised. If the thumbnail says `still funded`, the account is on screen inside the first thirty seconds. If it says `chop proof`, the chart is up. A hook that talks about a number without showing it breaks the pair.

### The body

Alexei's arc runs under every video regardless of length: problem, struggle, discovery, result. Six minutes or forty, the shape is the same.

Structure it as bullets, never as sentences. Five to nine bullets, each one a thing he knows cold. He reads the bullet, looks up, and talks. The moment it is written as prose he performs it, and a trader performing a script is the fastest way to lose a trader watching.

Open the loop early and close it before the end. Alexei is explicit that an unresolved loop costs trust on the next video, not just this one.

Reference the mechanism in every video. Niksetting's version of this is the useful one: indirect beats direct. The mechanism is whatever repeatable thing keeps five accounts alive at once. He should name it once, then stimulate it indirectly in every other video rather than pitching it. When someone eventually asks what he does, they already know.

Where he can, film undocumented rather than documented. Niksetting's cleanest distinction: documented is sitting down to record, undocumented is doing the work with the camera on. Same information, higher authority, and it removes the ideation time that is currently making him repeat the same video three times.

### The CTA

All three of Alexei's types belong in every video:

1. **Indirect.** Buried in the story. "the rule I run across all five accounts" told as narrative, never as an ask. This is the one that does the trust work.
2. **Soft.** Mid-video, framed as an optional resource for whoever wants the next step.
3. **Hard.** Last 60 to 90 seconds, explicit, tells the viewer exactly what to do.

Ayman's outro rule: never end on "that's everything for this video." The outro gives two doors, one for the viewer who is ready now and one for the viewer who needs another video first, and it hands the second viewer a specific next title rather than a shrug.

> Gap: we do not know whether he sells anything. The CTA structure above is correct regardless, but the hard CTA has no destination until he tells us what he offers, if anything. Do not write him a hard CTA before we know. Ask on the reply.

## Video skeletons

Bullets to read off, not scripts. Each one is his material, so he can leave the page whenever he wants.

**P1. `how i lost $20k in one week (full breakdown)`**
- Hook: the drawdown on screen, said plainly, plus the loop. "Here is every decision that got me there, and the one rule I wrote on the Monday after."
- The week before it went wrong: size, cadence, what was working
- The first red day and what he told himself that morning
- The decision that turned one red day into a red week
- What it cost, shown on the platform
- The rule he wrote afterwards
- The following week under that rule
- What he would tell someone sitting in week two of a drawdown right now
- CTA: indirect throughout (the rule is the mechanism), soft at the midpoint, hard in the last minute

**P2. `5 funded accounts, $2k payouts each (full breakdown)`**
- Hook: the payout cadence stated, the accounts on screen, loop opened on the one rule that does most of the work
- Why five accounts instead of one large one
- What is identical across all five
- The daily risk cap per account
- What happens the day one of them goes red
- The payout cadence and what he does with the money
- The mistake that blows accounts at exactly this stage
- CTA: this is the strongest MOF video on the channel, so the soft CTA carries the most weight here

**P3. `the risk plan that gets me 4r on nq (step by step)`**
- Hook: the chart up, the box drawn, the ratio named in the first sentence
- The conditions that have to be true before he even looks
- Where the entry goes
- Where the stop goes and the reasoning most people get wrong
- How the target gets set
- What he does when it moves against him immediately
- The version of this setup that does not work, and the filter that catches it
- CTA: hard CTA lands well here because the viewer has just been given something usable

**B1. `stop trading after a red week, do this instead (copy me)`**
- Hook: the Monday after a red week is what actually kills funded accounts, and here is the routine he runs instead of trading it
- What that Monday feels like, honestly
- What he used to do
- The routine now, specific and hour by hour
- When he is allowed back on full size
- How he tells the difference between ready and forcing
- CTA: TOF video, so lean indirect and soft, keep the hard CTA to a single line

**B2. `you don't need more setups, you need one (full breakdown)`**
- Hook: he trades one setup, here is everything he deleted and what happened to the numbers after
- What his chart looked like before
- Everything he removed, named specifically
- The one setup that survived and why that one
- What he gave up by simplifying, stated honestly
- How long before the numbers moved
- How someone else picks their one
- CTA: TOF, same weighting as B1

## Production notes

Trading audiences on funded accounts are money-motivated and time-poor, so run the B2B editing standard from [[content-engine/packaging-rules]]: no jump cuts, no heavy music, clear speech, minimal effects. Production flair reads as a content farm to someone deciding whether to trust a stranger with risk advice.

Every rebuilt title needs a hook that confirms the promise inside the first thirty seconds, not a setup. For P1 that means opening on the loss, not on how his week started.

Backgrounds move off the bed. Rule 4 of the thumbnail set wants a background that signals authority, and a bedroom with a closet in shot is the single fastest way to read as an amateur at 120px.
